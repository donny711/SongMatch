LLM Council Report — Session 2

Which option resolves the Deezer API violation: replace provider, seek partnership, or go non-commercial?

SongMatch · Solo developer · June 8, 2026
🚨 Apple Developer Account Risk — Surfaced by Peer Review

Apple's App Store Review Guidelines (5.2.2, 4.2) independently prohibit distributing audio without proper licensing — separate from Deezer's ToS. The consequence is not a rejected build. It is Apple Developer account termination, which ends your ability to ship any iOS app, ever. This risk exists regardless of whether you remove ads and subscriptions.

⚠ Option 3 May Not Be Compliant Either

No one on the council verified whether App Store distribution itself triggers Deezer's commercial use clause, independent of monetization. "Commercial use" may be defined by distribution channel, not revenue. Going non-commercial may not reduce legal exposure at all — it just removes revenue.

The One Thing to Do First

Spend $300–$500 on one hour with a music licensing attorney who understands App Store distribution. You need to confirm: (1) whether a free App Store app still violates Deezer's commercial use clause, and (2) whether Apple's guidelines independently bar this app in its current form. Every other action before that conversation is potentially wasted work or compounded legal exposure.

Chairman's Verdict

📋 Where the Council Agrees

Stop building on Deezer. The violation is active today, not theoretical. Every advisor reached this conclusion through different reasoning — which makes it more reliable. Option 2 is unanimously eliminated. Revenue-first thinking at zero users is the wrong sequence. The Expansionist was the outlier and the peer review unanimously identified their response as the weakest — the Spotify acquisition argument collapses because Spotify's SDK explicitly prohibits standalone apps that compete with Spotify's own discovery features.

⚡ Where the Council Clashes

Is Option 3 a safe harbor or a quieter violation? The Contrarian says "commercial use" follows intent, not price — a free app built to monetize later may still be prohibited. The Executor says it has a clear Monday action. The Outsider calls it the only honest answer. They're all arguing about the same option without resolving the definitional question — which is why the lawyer recommendation is the actual first step.

Is Option 1 viable at this scale? The Expansionist says yes (Spotify). The First Principles Thinker says no — structurally impossible for an indie dev. The peer review added a third path nobody named: SoundCloud and Audiomack, which sit between "impossible" and "fantasy."

🔍 Blind Spots the Council Caught

Apple Developer account termination (caught by reviewers 1, 3, 5): This is categorically more serious than Deezer API termination. Losing API access is a product problem. Losing your Apple Developer account is an existential problem for your iOS career.

Option 3 compliance is unverified (reviewer 2): The council unanimously recommended Option 3 without anyone checking whether App Store distribution itself triggers Deezer's commercial use clause. That is a significant analytical gap.

Alternative providers overlooked (reviewer 4): SoundCloud and Audiomack both offer developer APIs with commercial licensing at materially lower barriers than Spotify. Nobody mentioned them.

Data continuity ignored (reviewer 4): If Deezer terminates access without notice, existing user taste profiles become orphaned overnight. The core product asset disappears with no warning.

✅ The Recommendation

Do not choose an option until you have confirmed two facts you do not currently know:

1. Does Deezer's commercial use clause apply to free App Store distribution with no monetization? If yes, Option 3 does not reduce legal risk.

2. What is your exposure under Apple's App Store guidelines? Losing your developer account is permanent. Get this confirmed before submitting anything.

If legal review confirms Option 3 satisfies both: strip monetization immediately, ship, build users, then approach SoundCloud or Audiomack for commercial licensing once you have proof of traction. If Option 3 does not satisfy the compliance tests: the app cannot ship in its current form. Replace Deezer with SoundCloud or Audiomack — not Spotify.

Full Advisor Responses

Get a lawyerThe Contrarian

Option 3 is a trap disguised as the safe choice. Removing monetization doesn't actually solve the problem — it just makes the violation quieter. "Commercial use" typically encompasses apps distributed through commercial channels, apps that drive user acquisition for future monetization, and apps that build commercial brand equity. A free app on the App Store with the explicit intent to monetize later is still commercial use. You're not safe — you're just less obviously unsafe.

Option 2 is wishful thinking. Deezer has no incentive to negotiate a custom commercial license with an indie developer whose app competes with their own discovery features.

Option 1 has a buried fatal flaw: Spotify's SDK has been notoriously restrictive and has killed apps in review. Apple Music API requires users to have an Apple Music subscription. Neither gives you the clean "30-second preview for any user" mechanic.

The real question being avoided: is the core mechanic legally viable at commercial scale with any provider? The developer needs a music licensing lawyer before choosing any option. The three options assume the product is viable. That assumption hasn't been verified.

Option 3 (intentional)The First Principles Thinker

The question is wrong. Strip everything away. Does a viable licensed audio preview solution that makes unit economics work even exist? Spotify's SDK terms prohibit standalone apps. Apple Music API requires existing subscriptions. Direct licensing for an indie developer is a multi-year legal and financial undertaking. Option 1 may be structurally impossible at this scale. Option 2 is not a plan.

Option 3 is the only honest answer — but reframe it: go non-commercial intentionally, not as a retreat. Ship it. Build real users. Build proof of concept with traction. That traction is your only negotiating leverage with any licensor. Revenue-first thinking at zero users is the wrong sequence entirely.

Option 1 (Spotify)The Expansionist

Option 1, but think bigger. The taste profile data is the asset — Spotify paid $170M for The Echo Nest. Pick Spotify. Their partner SDK gives you 30-second previews legally, their developer program is mature, and Spotify has been acquiring music discovery apps. Build something Spotify wants to acquire.

Option 3 signals low ambition and removes the financial pressure that forces good product decisions. Option 2 is small thinking — Deezer is a shrinking platform. The API violation is a forcing function to upgrade your ambitions. Use it.

Note: Peer review unanimously flagged this as the weakest response. Spotify's SDK explicitly prohibits standalone discovery apps that compete with Spotify's own features.

Option 3 (phase 1)The Outsider

Option 3 is the only honest answer right now, but not as a permanent destination. The app does not exist as a viable business yet — no users, no revenue, no proof. You cannot lose money you have never made.

Options 1 and 2 both require the app to keep running while you solve a legal problem. That is backwards. Go non-commercial immediately. Ship. Get real users. Get real feedback.

Once you have user data, Option 2 becomes a much stronger conversation with Deezer — you are showing them a live app with demonstrated traction. And Option 1 becomes easier to justify. The monetization features are paused, not lost. The legal risk is real and present. Those are not equivalent concerns.

Option 3 (now)The Executor

Option 3 is the only one with a clear Monday morning action. Option 1: you wait for undefined SDK access timelines. Option 2: you wait for a response that statistically will not come.

Option 3: Remove AdMob SDK calls. Remove RevenueCat paywalls. Submit to App Store. Done in days, not months.

You're in violation today — Deezer can cut API access without notice, killing the app and your data entirely. Option 3 lets you ship a working, compliant product immediately. Once live and compliant, pursue Options 1 or 2 in parallel from a position of stability. Go non-commercial now. The revenue streams you're protecting don't exist if API access gets terminated tomorrow.
